Principia Metaphysica · a primer for beginners

The 26-dimensional model, from the ground up

Principia Metaphysica is a speculative model of physics. It has not been validated, and its fit to measurements is poor today. This page explains its ideas with school-level maths and pictures, and says which parts are worked out, which are assumed and which fail.

The idea
Space has hidden dimensions curled into small shapes. The holes in those shapes are meant to set the particles and forces we see.
Worked out
The model's central formula (its action) has now been solved. It gives gravity. It does not give the known forces or particle families.
Not settled
A second layer of formulas is tested directly against data. Its score is poor and is being re-counted after an audit found 540 defects.

1Dimensions you cannot see

You can move left and right, forward and back, up and down. Add time and you have the four dimensions of everyday physics. The model says there are more: 26 in total, 24 of space and 2 of time.

Picture it

A garden hose seen from across a field looks like a line. An ant on the hose knows better: it can walk along the hose and also around it. A hidden dimension is the "around" direction, curled so small that we only notice the "along".

The model arranges its 26 dimensions in a particular way. It splits them into two halves of 13, which it calls shadows. Each shadow has 12 dimensions of space and 1 of time. Inside one shadow, 4 dimensions are the world we see and 9 are hidden.

The maths

26 = 13 + 13. Each 13 = 12 + 1 (space plus time), and each 13 = 4 + 2 + 7. The matter field of the model is a spinor, a kind of list of numbers at every point. In D dimensions (D even) it has 2D/2 entries: 213 = 8192. The model keeps one handedness, which is half: 4096.

Status

The split of 26 into two halves with one time each is assumed. The model borrows a symmetry called Sp(2,R) to do it and has not derived that symmetry from its own equations. Two earlier arguments, one for why the number is 26 and one for why two times are safe, have been withdrawn.

2Why the hidden shape matters

Picture it

A guitar string can only play certain notes, because a wave has to fit its length. A hidden dimension works the same way. A wave travelling around the hidden loop has to join up with itself, so only whole numbers of waves fit.

The maths

A loop of radius R has length 2πR. Fitting n waves needs nλ = 2πR. De Broglie's rule from school physics says momentum is p = h/λ, so p = nh/2πR = nħ/R. We cannot see motion around the loop. It shows up as mass: m = nħ/(Rc). A smaller loop gives heavier steps, which is why nobody would have seen them yet.

The step n = 0 has no mass at all. Those massless pieces are what everyday physics is made of, and how many there are depends on the holes in the hidden shape.

3Counting holes

Mathematicians tell shapes apart by counting their holes. Stretching and bending do not change the count, so it is a very robust number.

The maths

Take a cube and count corners minus edges plus faces: 8 − 12 + 6 = 2. Any solid you could inflate into a ball gives 2. Shapes with g holes give χ = 2 − 2g.

Shapes with more dimensions have more kinds of hole. They are counted by the Betti numbers b1, b2, b3 and so on. b1 counts loops that cannot be shrunk to a point: none on a sphere, two on a doughnut (around the tube, and around the hole). b2 and b3 count the same thing for two- and three-dimensional surfaces inside the shape.

The model's 7-dimensional shape was built by the mathematician Dominic Joyce in 1996. Start with a 7-dimensional torus. Fold it using three symmetries. The folding leaves 12 creases. Smooth each crease.

The maths

Before smoothing, b2 = 0 and b3 = 7. Each of the 12 smoothed creases adds 1 to b2 and 3 to b3. So b2 = 12 and b3 = 7 + 3 × 12 = 43. These two whole numbers are the model's starting data.

Status

The model then guesses that the number of particle families is b2/4 = 3. Nature does have three families (the electron, the muon and the tau, each with relatives). The division by 4 is a chosen rule. Stop 7 shows that the model's own central formula does not produce it.

4Curvature and the flat torus

Curvature measures how far a surface is from flat. You can test it by drawing a triangle and adding up its angles.

A theorem of Gauss and Bonnet links curvature to holes. However you dent or stretch a closed surface, its curvature added up over the whole surface depends only on the number of holes.

The maths

Total curvature = 2πχ = 4π(1 − g). Check it on a sphere of radius r: the curvature is 1/r2 everywhere and the area is 4πr2, so the total is 4π, as the formula says for g = 0.

The model's equations for empty space demand zero curvature at every point of the hidden 2-dimensional piece. Zero everywhere means zero in total, so 4π(1 − g) = 0 and g = 1. Only one shape passes.

Picture it

A flat torus is the screen of an old arcade game. Fly off the right edge and you come back on the left. Fly off the top and you come back at the bottom. The screen is perfectly flat, and yet it has the hole count of a doughnut.

Status

Until this audit the model had not said which 2-dimensional shape it uses. The owner's decision of 11 November 2026 makes the flat torus the default. The sphere stays in the code as a switchable option, where it fails this check.

5The master action

Physics has a compact way to state a theory. You write one quantity, the action, and declare that nature follows the path that makes it stationary (usually smallest). Every equation of motion follows from that one line.

Picture it

A lifeguard sees a swimmer in trouble. Running is faster than swimming, so the quickest route is a bent one: more sand, less water. Light does the same thing when it enters glass. The action is the quantity being minimised, here the travel time.

The maths

For a thrown ball the action is S = ∫ (½mv2 − mgh) dt: kinetic energy minus potential energy, added up over the flight. Asking for the path that makes S stationary gives back a = −g, which is Newton's law for the ball.

The model's master action has the same structure, with the geometry of 26 dimensions in place of the ball:

S = ∫ d26X √|G| [ ½ M*24 R + Ψ iΓMDM Ψ ]

SymbolMeaning in plain words
∫ d26X √|G|Add up over every point of the 26 dimensions, counting each small cell by its volume.
RThe curvature of space and time. This term is gravity, as in Einstein's theory.
M*A mass scale that sets how stiff space is.
ΨThe matter field: the spinor with 4096 entries from stop 1.
ΓMDMHow the matter field changes from point to point. This is its motion term.

Solving the action means four steps, each of which can fail:

  1. Derive the equations of motion.
  2. Find a solution for empty space (the vacuum).
  3. List the massless fields that live on that vacuum. These are the particles.
  4. Compare with experiment.

The project did this in November 2026. Stops 6 to 8 are the results.

6Two clocks and a term that cancels

The first result is about the matter term. Particles like the electron come in two mirror versions, left-handed and right-handed. The model's matter field has one handedness only.

Picture it

Every term in the action is a handshake between two copies of the field. With one time dimension, the motion term is a handshake between two copies of the same hand, so a one-handed field can have it. A second time dimension adds one more flip. The motion term then needs a left hand and a right hand, and a one-handed field has no partner.

Term for a one-handed fieldOne time (everyday physics)Two times (this model)
Motion term✓ allowed✗ exactly zero
Mass term✗ exactly zero✓ allowed
The maths

The calculation ends with the term equal to minus itself: x = −x, so 2x = 0 and x = 0. The general rule: the usual motion term for a one-handed field exists when the number of time dimensions is odd, and vanishes when it is even.

So the matter term, as the model had written it, was zero. The project's code had the rule the wrong way round in five places, copied from one-time physics. The repair is a different way of pairing the field with itself, called the holomorphic form. It does not vanish in 26 dimensions.

The maths

The holomorphic form works when the number of dimensions leaves remainder 2 on division by 8. 26 = 3 × 8 + 2, so it does. The project checked both statements by direct calculation with the full 8192 × 8192 matrices, and on six smaller test cases.

Status

Owner's decision of 11 November 2026: the non-vanishing form is the default. The old form stays in the code as a switch, off by default, in case it is needed later.

7What the solved action gives

In 1921 Theodor Kaluza found something remarkable: gravity in five dimensions looks, in four, like gravity plus electromagnetism. The force comes from a symmetry of the hidden shape.

Handedness is the second problem. The weak force treats left-handed and right-handed particles differently. A shadow has 13 dimensions, and in an odd number of dimensions a spinor has no handedness of its own. Curling up the hidden dimensions then gives left and right in equal numbers.

The maths

The 46 extra fields are b3 + 3 = 43 + 3. Each of the 43 three-dimensional holes of the G₂ shape can change size on its own, and the torus adds three more numbers: its area and two for its slant. The count b2 = 12 appears nowhere. The action has no field that can feel two-dimensional holes.

Status

These results are theorems and explicit calculations, so fixing bugs will not change them. Physicists met the same obstacles in the 1980s when they tried to get all forces from gravity in extra dimensions (Edward Witten's papers of 1981 and 1983). The model has now run into them by its own calculation. Choosing a different smooth 7-dimensional shape of the same kind would not help. The action needs more ingredients, or the shape needs creases.

8A size that nothing fixes

Picture it

Think of a balloon whose rubber has no stretchiness at all. Nothing sets its size, so it can be a little bigger here and a little smaller there at no cost. In the solved action the hidden dimensions are like that. Their overall size is one of the 46 massless fields, free to vary from place to place.

A freely varying size acts like an extra part of gravity, and that can be tested. Einstein's theory says how much the Sun bends light, through a number called γ. General relativity predicts γ = 1. In 2003 radio signals from the Cassini spacecraft, passing close to the Sun, measured it.

The maths

With n hidden dimensions of free size, the reduced theory has a parameter ω = −1 + 1/n. The light-bending number is γ = 1 + ω2 + ω = 1/n1 + 1/n = 1n + 1. For n = 9 that is 1/10. The measurement is γ − 1 = (2.1 ± 2.3) × 10−5.

Status

Taken as the whole theory, the action is ruled out by this one measurement. The model needs something that pins the size of the hidden dimensions, which physicists call a potential. The adopted action has none. The project now records this as a failed prediction and keeps it on the books.

9Two layers and the scoreboard

The model's published numbers (the strength of forces, particle masses, mixing angles) do not come from solving the action. They come from a second layer: formulas whose form was chosen, built from the hole counts 12 and 43 and the family number 3. A chosen form like this is called an ansatz. Each formula is tested directly against a measurement.

How a prediction is scored

Every measurement comes with an uncertainty. The score of a prediction is how many uncertainties it sits away from the measured value. Physicists call that number σ (sigma).

The maths

σ = |prediction − measurement| / uncertainty. The overall score, chi-square, adds the squares: χ2 = σ12 + σ22 + … For a good fit, χ2 comes out near the number of rows.

The maths

One row explains the giant totals. The model's value for the fine-structure constant (the strength of electromagnetism, about 1/137) sits 33,457σ from the measurement, and 33,4572 ≈ 1.12 × 109. The same quantity was listed under two names, which doubles it to 2.24 × 109. That constant is measured to better than one part in a billion, so a formula that misses by a few parts in a million lands tens of thousands of σ away. Even the best case is poor: √(12,649 / 48) ≈ 16, an average miss of 16σ per row.

Status

The audit found 540 defects in the code, paper and website. Of the confirmed ones, 62 are critical and 174 high. They include quantities scored twice, measured values typed in and then scored as predictions, and numbers left over from an older choice of shape. The fine-structure formula may itself contain a setting tuned for that older shape. The score will be re-counted when the fixes are in. Nothing on this scale turns a 16σ average into a good fit, so the verdict of a poor fit stands for now.

10What would change the picture

The solved action shows exactly which ingredients are missing. Each one becomes an option that can be switched on and evaluated. None is adopted unless it comes with a definition, a source for its strength and a check that can fail.

Status

The crease idea comes from M-theory, a different theory in 11 dimensions, where creases of exactly this kind carry forces and special points carry one-handed matter (Acharya 1998; Atiyah and Witten 2001). The number 12 in this model is the number of creases. Whether anything similar holds in a 26-dimensional theory with two times is not known. It is a lead to investigate, and the project does not claim it as a result.

How the model can be shown wrong

  • Every scored row is a prediction with a measurement next to it. Rows that fail stay on the books, marked as falsified. Nothing is deleted.
  • The light-bending result of stop 8 is a test the bare action has already failed.
  • The model has not yet committed to an ordering of the neutrino masses. Its two earlier statements contradicted each other, so the question is marked undecided until the fixes are done.

What happens next in the project

Words used on this page

Action
One quantity that defines a theory. Nature follows the path that makes it stationary.
Ansatz
A formula whose form is chosen and then tested, as opposed to derived.
Betti number
A count of holes of a given dimension in a shape.
Chi-square (χ²)
The sum of all σ² values. Near the number of rows for a good fit.
G₂ manifold
A 7-dimensional shape with zero Ricci curvature and a special kind of symmetry in how directions rotate as you move around it.
Graviton
The particle of gravity.
Handedness
Whether a particle spins like a left-handed or a right-handed screw. Also called chirality.
Potential
An energy cost that pushes a field toward a preferred value.
Ricci-flat
Having zero curvature of the kind that enters Einstein's equations for empty space.
Sigma (σ)
The distance between prediction and measurement, counted in uncertainties.
Spinor
The mathematical object that describes matter particles such as the electron.
Torus
The surface of a doughnut. A flat torus is a rectangle with opposite edges joined.
